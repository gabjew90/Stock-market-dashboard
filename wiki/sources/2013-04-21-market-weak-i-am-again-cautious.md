# WW 2013-04-21 — Never buy a stock that has been much higher — overhead supply as a prohibition

**Source:** [Market weak; I am again cautious](../../raw/posts/2013-04-21-market-weak-i-am-again-cautious.md) · [original](https://wishingwealthblog.com/2013/04/market-weak-i-am-again-cautious/) · tier: teaching

## What it covers

Answering a reader on whether gold or silver were good buys, he states the rule that follows from the
overhead-supply argument — and it is a prohibition, not a preference: "**As you know, I only buy stocks or ETF's
that are near or above their green line tops. I do not buy stocks that have been much higher and declined. There
are so many people waiting to sell as the stock comes back, and one never knows if the decline will resume. A
failed come back rally can lead to a quick, vicious decline.**" The trapped sellers are the mechanism; the risk
is that the recovery attempt fails *into* them. **And the failed-GLB tell applied to a Dow stock**: on IBM's
post-earnings collapse, "**the only technical tip off I might have noticed is that it failed its recent green
line break-out several times. When a stock bursts through a multi-month green line base to an all-time high, it
should not look back much. IBM closed below its green line top on several occasions.**" So repeated failures at
the line are themselves the warning — the same information the exit rule uses, read before owning the stock.

## Key claims

- **"I do not buy stocks that have been much higher and declined"** — the overhead-supply argument as a prohibition on bottom-fishing, applied to ETFs as well as stocks.
- **"A failed come back rally can lead to a quick, vicious decline"** — the specific risk, which is why a recovering stock is worse than a flat one.
- **Repeated GLB failures are a tell**: "when a stock bursts through a multi-month green line base to an all-time high, it should not look back much."
- IBM read as a case that could have been avoided by watching the line rather than the earnings.
- The earnings-season pattern restated: strength into earnings, then a post-earnings lull.
